How Do Deserts Form?

When you hear the word "desert," you might imagine endless sand dunes under a blazing Sun. But deserts are much more interesting than that — and they don't all look the same. So how does a place become a desert in the first place?

🏜️ A Desert Is About Rainfall

The most important feature of a desert is not its sand. It is the lack of precipitation.

Deserts receive very little precipitation compared with most other environments. Some deserts are sandy, but others are covered by rocks, gravel, salt flats or even ice.

A desert does not have to be hot or covered in sand. The key feature is extreme dryness.

☀️ How Does the Atmosphere Create Dry Areas?

One major way deserts form is through global atmospheric circulation.

Near the equator, strong heating causes warm, moist air to rise. As this air moves higher, it cools and can release much of its moisture as rain.

The now-drier air eventually moves downward in some subtropical regions. Descending air tends to make cloud formation and rainfall more difficult.

Less rising moist air + less rainfall = conditions that can support desert formation.

🏔️ Mountains Can Create Deserts

Mountains can force moist air to rise. As the air rises, it cools and may release precipitation on one side of the mountain range.

By the time the air reaches the other side, it may contain much less moisture.

This dry region is called a rain shadow and can become a desert over time.

🌊 Cold Ocean Currents

Some deserts form near coastlines where cold ocean currents flow.

Cold water cools the air above it. This can reduce evaporation and make it harder for the atmosphere to produce significant rainfall over nearby land.

As a result, extremely dry coastal environments can exist even beside an ocean.

🌍 Some Deserts Are Far From the Ocean

Distance from the ocean can also contribute to dryness. Air travelling far over land may lose much of its moisture before reaching inland regions.

This is one reason some large continental interiors can have very dry climates.

❄️ Not All Deserts Are Hot

A desert is defined mainly by its dryness, not by its temperature.

Antarctica is actually the world's largest desert because it receives extremely little precipitation.

It is a cold desert, covered mainly by ice rather than sand.

Hot desert ≠ all deserts.
Dryness is what matters most.

🌵 How Do Plants Survive There?

Even extremely dry deserts can support life. Plants and animals that live there have adaptations that help them conserve and find water.

Some plants store water, have reduced leaves, or have specialized root systems. Many desert animals are adapted to avoid the hottest parts of the day.

⏳ Deserts Can Change Over Time

Deserts are not completely unchanging landscapes. Climate patterns can shift over thousands or millions of years.

Changes in rainfall, temperature, atmospheric circulation and geography can make regions become wetter or drier.

✨ Quick Facts

🏜️ Deserts are defined mainly by very low precipitation.
🏔️ Mountains can create dry rain-shadow regions behind them.
❄️ Antarctica is a desert even though it is covered in ice.

💡 The Big Idea

Deserts form when an area receives so little precipitation that the environment becomes extremely dry.

Global air circulation, mountains, cold ocean currents and distance from moisture sources can all contribute to desert formation.

So a desert isn't necessarily a giant pile of sand. It is a place where water is scarce.
